How Expensive Is the
Stock Market?

A few questions worth asking about your savings, before the cycle answers them for you.
 

The questions matter more than the answers.

You earned every dollar of your savings, and forward progress is the name of the game. But anyone who has lived long enough knows there are cycles to everything, the rising and the falling. The trick is not predicting the cycle. The trick is recognizing where you stand inside it.

Most real estate people we know will tell you the same thing: you win on the purchase, not on the sale. Price matters. Where you sit in the cycle matters more.

So here is one question worth sitting with. If all your retirement savings were in cash today, would you choose to buy the stock market at its current price? And if the answer is no, it is too high, then the follow-up asks itself. Why stay there tomorrow?

A note before we continue

Nothing in this letter is financial advice. These are questions, the kind a thoughtful friend would ask before you wrote a check. If they cause you to pause, that is the entire purpose.

 

The price of the market, measured three ways

You do not need to take our word for any of this. All three measures are in the public record, and all three are dated. Here they are.

Measure one

Price against ten years of earnings

The Cyclically Adjusted Price-to-Earnings ratio, known as the CAPE or the Shiller PE, measures the market's price against ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings. Smoothing a full decade strips out the good quarters and the bad ones. It is the work that helped earn Robert Shiller the Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences in 2013.

Here is where that measure stood in May 2026, next to where it stood before three events people still remember.

In 145 years of records, the reading has closed above 40 in only two periods. December 1999 was the first. May 2026 is the second.

Put plainly: in May 2026 the market was priced at more than two and a half times its own long-run median. The all-time high in the series, 44.19, was set in December 1999.

Source: Shiller PE (CAPE) for the S&P 500. Robert Shiller, Yale University, via multpl.com. Readings are month-end. Other providers using slightly different earnings inputs published May 2026 readings between 39.9 and 41.6. Past performance is not indicative of future results.

Who is Robert Shiller?

Nobel laureate in Economic Sciences, 2013, for empirical analysis of asset prices. Sterling Professor Emeritus of Economics at Yale and Professor of Finance at the Yale School of Management. Doctorate in economics from MIT.

He identified the dot-com bubble in print before it broke, and the housing bubble before that one broke. He is a co-creator of the S&P CoreLogic Case-Shiller Home Price Indices and the author of Irrational Exuberance.

Measure two

The index priced in dollars

This is the chart everyone shows you. It is the one your statement reflects, and the one cable news points to when it says the market is healthy.

Measured this way, twenty-six years look like one long climb.

Source: S&P 500 index level, closing high of 1,527 on March 24, 2000 and the close at the end of May 2026. Price return, dividends excluded. Past performance is not indicative of future results.
Measure three

The index priced in ounces of gold

The chart in dollars hides one thing, and it is the important thing. It does not show you what is happening to the unit of measurement itself.

The math is simple. Take the index level and divide it by the price of one ounce of gold. That tells you how many ounces it takes to buy the index. Same index, measured in something that cannot be created with a keystroke.

Same companies. Same twenty-six years. A different ruler.

Gold did not become more valuable by itself. The dollars used to measure it became less valuable. The chart is the receipt.

Basis: the S&P 500 to gold ratio, about 5.40 ounces at the March 2000 peak to about 1.64 ounces at the end of May 2026 (MacroTrends; DWS Asset Management; month-end gold close). Past performance is not indicative of future results.

Two of these charts describe the same twenty-six years. Only the ruler changed.

 

Watch what the money does

Warren Buffett stepped down as chief executive of Berkshire Hathaway on December 31, 2025. Greg Abel runs the company now.

What has not changed is the cash. Berkshire closed the first quarter of 2026 holding a record $397.4 billion in cash, cash equivalents and short-term Treasury bills, above the previous record of $381.7 billion set in the third quarter of 2025. The company has been a net seller of stocks for fourteen consecutive quarters. That is roughly a third of everything Berkshire owns, sitting in Treasury bills.

Buffett built that position over his last years running the company. His successor has not spent it.

Source: Berkshire Hathaway Inc., Form 10-Q for the first quarter of 2026. Figures are cash, cash equivalents and short-term investments in U.S. Treasury bills. Nothing here should be read as a recommendation to follow any investor's positioning.

A quiet test

Has the firm holding your savings called you to talk about any of this?

If the answer is no, it is fair to ask why. So pick up the phone and call whoever manages your account. Ask one question:

"Given where the Shiller PE sits, and given Berkshire's record cash position, what is your view on my exposure?"

Whatever answer you get, or do not get, will tell you something useful.

 

Why physical metal, specifically

Physical gold sits outside the financial plumbing that occasionally seizes up. It is held in a depository or in your own hands, not as a line on a brokerage statement. It does not depend on a counterparty staying solvent, a clearing system staying healthy, or a software update going well on a Sunday night.

It also carries its own risks. Its price rises and falls, it pays no dividend and no interest, and it can sit flat for years. We would rather you hear that from us than discover it later.

A final set of questions

Take five minutes. Answer them honestly, to yourself. None of them is a prediction. They are planning questions.

1.  Do you know what your downside is in dollars, not in percentages?
2.  If your account balance fell by a third, what would that change about your retirement, your home, your family?
3.  Has anyone shown you what share of your savings sits in one asset class?
4.  And finally: are you sitting courtside, or in the nosebleed seats?

Nosebleed seats

Picture the arena. Down at courtside, close enough to hear the squeak of the shoes, is where money sits when it was bought right, at the bottom of the cycle, with room to rise.

The nosebleed seats are the other end. Up where the air is thin. You did not choose those seats. The cycle walked you up there, row by row, while the statements kept saying everything was fine.

Look back at what you have just read. A valuation reading above 40 for only the second time in 145 years. A record cash position at Berkshire. The same index down about 70 percent when you measure it in gold. None of that is a forecast. It is the scoreboard as of May 2026.

You cannot pick the cycle. You can pick your seat.
