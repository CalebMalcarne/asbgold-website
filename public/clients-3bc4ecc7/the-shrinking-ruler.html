A SHORT GUIDE FOR THE PEOPLE WE'VE ALREADY SPOKEN WITH

The Shrinking
Ruler

Why your savings can look the same on paper and still be quietly losing ground.
 

Pull a $20 bill out of your wallet. Look at it.

It looks exactly like the $20 bill you carried twenty years ago. Same color. Same portrait. Same number in the corner.

But it isn't the same.

In 2005, that $20 filled your gas tank. Today it gets you most of the way through one trip to the grocery store. The bill didn't change. What it can do has changed.

This is the single most important thing to understand about cash savings in retirement, and almost nobody explains it plainly. So we will.

The dollar is a ruler that keeps getting shorter. A shorter ruler makes everything around it look taller.

When the price of a home goes from $200,000 to $400,000, the home didn't double in value. The ruler shrank.

When the stock market hits record highs year after year, part of that "high" isn't growth at all. It's the same companies, measured with a smaller unit.

And when your bank statement shows the same balance it did five years ago, that balance is not standing still. It is sinking.

 

The proof, three ways

You don't need to take our word for any of this. The math is in the public record. Here it is in three short pieces.

Proof #1

The official inflation number

According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the dollar has lost roughly a third of its purchasing power since 2005. A $500,000 cash balance from twenty years ago buys what about $310,000 would buy today.

Same number on the statement. Two-thirds the life.

Proof #2

The money supply

The amount of dollars in circulation has roughly quadrupled since 2000. When the supply of anything expands faster than the demand for it, each individual unit is worth less.

Dollars are not exempt from that rule. They have never been exempt from it.

Proof #3

The stock market, measured honestly

Since the year 2000, the S&P 500 is up roughly +388% in dollars. Measured in ounces of gold instead of dollars, the same index is down about 70%.

Basis: S&P 500 index level, closing high of 1,527 on March 24, 2000 to about 7,460 at the end of May 2026. The S&P 500-to-gold ratio, about 5.4 ounces at the March 2000 peak to about 1.66 ounces in November 2025 (MacroTrends; DWS Asset Management). Past performance is not indicative of future results.

Same companies. Same quarter-century. Two completely different stories, depending on which ruler you use to measure.

The number on your statement is not the same thing as what that number can do.
 

Why this is the quiet danger

A stock market crash makes the front page. You see it. You feel it. You can react to it.

Inflation does not make the front page. It works one cent at a time, one grocery trip at a time, one insurance renewal at a time. You don't feel it in any single moment. You feel it when you look up one day and realize the life your savings was supposed to fund is suddenly more expensive than the savings can cover.

That is the danger of a shrinking ruler. By the time the loss is obvious, it has already happened.

 

What gold actually is

Gold is not an investment in the way most people use that word. We don't talk about it as a way to get rich. That's not what it does, and that's not why central banks, sovereign wealth funds, and serious families have held it for centuries.

Gold is the one form of savings that cannot be diluted by anyone.

No central bank can print more of it. No government can change its definition. No policy decision can quietly cut its supply in half.

The ounce you hold is the same ounce a Roman senator held, the same ounce held during the depression, the same ounce that will exist a hundred years from now.

That is the whole point. It isn't a bet on the future. It is a measurement that doesn't shrink.

You worked forty years for that money. You don't need to get rich from it.
You need it to still be worth something when you need it.
